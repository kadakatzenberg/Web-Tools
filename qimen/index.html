    02

    The Turtle's
    Back

    洛書

    Before the gates, before the stars, there is a square of nine numbers. Legend puts it on the shell of a turtle rising from the Luo river; mathematics puts it as the unique 3×3 magic square. Qi Men treats it as the floor plan of space itself.

    1. I

      Nine numbers, one to nine, each used once.

    2. II

      Every row, every column, both diagonals — fifteen.

    3. III

      Odd numbers hold the poles and the centre; even numbers take the corners. Yang is the cross, yin is the X.

    4. IV

      Each cell becomes a palace: a direction, a trigram, an element, an hour of the day and a stretch of a life.

    5. V

      Energy does not walk the square in reading order. It walks 1→2→3…→9 by number, which traces a path across space that looks like nothing at all — and that path is the engine.

    後天八卦 · Hòu Tiān Bā Guà

    The square is laid over the compass. North sits at the bottom — the Chinese convention, and the one Qi Men keeps. Each palace now carries a trigram, an element, a family member, a season, and a temperament.

    Reading the grid

    Palace 5 is the centre. It has no trigram and no door, because it is not a place you can stand — it is the axis the other eight turn around. When anything is forced into the centre, tradition lodges it in palace 2 instead. That single exception generates most of the asymmetry in the system.

      What the Emperor Hides

      遁甲 · 三奇六儀
      Ten stems, nine palaces
      one of them never appears
      甲

      Jia — 甲 — is the first of the ten heavenly stems, and in this system it stands for the sovereign. A sovereign on an open field is a target. So Qi Men does something no other Chinese art does: it removes him from the board.

      Jia takes shelter behind six escorts, the 六儀 Six Yi, borrowing whichever one leads the current ten-hour period. Look for him in a chart and you will never find him. You find the escort, and you infer the king. That act of concealment is the Dun Jia in Qi Men Dun Jia — and it is why the art was historically a military one.
